# Voice & Tone

Framework for capturing, documenting, and adapting brand voice across content types.

## Core Voice Statement

Define your voice in 2-3 sentences that capture the essence — not a list of adjectives, but the *feeling* a reader gets. This is the north star for all content.

**Template:** "[Brand] writes like [positioning]. We [key behavior] and [key behavior]. Our readers feel [emotional outcome]."

**Example:** "Acme writes like a sharp colleague who's been there. We explain complex ideas in plain language and back claims with data. Our readers feel informed without feeling talked down to."

## Voice Rules

### Always
- Specific over vague — name the thing, cite the number, show the example
- Active voice — "We reduced churn by 23%" not "Churn was reduced"
- Reader benefit first — lead with what they gain, not what you did
- Consistent register — match formality level across a piece

### Never
- Jargon without context — if a term needs explanation, explain it or cut it
- Passive hedging — "It could be argued that..." (just argue it)
- Corporate filler — "leverage synergies", "best-in-class", "holistic approach"
- Talking down — "Simply do X" (nothing is simple if you're learning it)

### Usually, Unless
- Short sentences (15-20 words) — unless a longer construction adds rhythm or clarity
- Second person ("you") — unless the content type calls for third person (press releases, case studies)
- Contractions — unless formality requires them out (legal, compliance)
- One idea per paragraph — unless building a deliberate argument chain

## Sentence Construction

| Element | Guideline |
|---------|-----------|
| **Preferred length** | 12-20 words; mix short punchy with longer explanatory |
| **Variety pattern** | Alternate short/medium/long; never 3+ same-length in a row |
| **Opening moves** | Lead with subject-verb, question, or bold claim — not dependent clauses |
| **Power positions** | Key info at sentence end (stress position) and paragraph start (topic position) |

### Sentence Openers to Vary
- **Subject-verb:** "Our team tested three approaches."
- **Prepositional:** "In Q3, conversion rates doubled."
- **Participial:** "Analyzing the data, we found a pattern."
- **Question:** "What happens when you remove friction from the signup flow?"
- **Imperative:** "Start with your highest-intent keywords."

## Word Selection

| Principle | Do | Don't |
|-----------|------|--------|
| **Concrete over abstract** | "Cut load time by 2 seconds" | "Improved performance significantly" |
| **Common over fancy** | "Use", "Start", "Build" | "Utilize", "Commence", "Construct" |
| **Precise over approximate** | "47% of B2B buyers" | "Many industry professionals" |
| **Active over passive** | "The team shipped the fix" | "The fix was shipped by the team" |
| **Fresh over cliche** | (varies by context) | "Game-changer", "Move the needle", "At the end of the day" |

### Banned Words/Phrases (Marketing Content)
- Synergy, leverage (as verb), holistic, best-in-class
- Revolutionary, game-changing, disruptive (unless literally true)
- Simply, just, obviously (dismissive)
- In today's fast-paced world (AI cliche)
- It goes without saying (then don't say it)

## Tone Adaptation

Voice stays constant. Tone shifts by context.

### By Content Type

| Content Type | Tone Shift | Example |
|-------------|-----------|---------|
| **Blog post** | Conversational, teacherly | "Here's what we found when we tested this..." |
| **Landing page** | Confident, benefit-focused | "Get more leads with less effort." |
| **Email** | Personal, direct | "Quick update on something that worked for us." |
| **Case study** | Professional, evidence-driven | "After implementing X, Company Y saw a 40% increase." |
| **Social media** | Punchy, engaging | "We ran 200 A/B tests. The winner surprised us." |
| **Press release** | Formal, factual | "Company announces partnership to expand..." |

### By Audience Emotional State

| State | Tone Approach | Avoid |
|-------|-------------|-------|
| **Frustrated** | Empathetic, solution-focused | Cheerfulness, dismissiveness |
| **Skeptical** | Evidence-heavy, transparent | Hype, unsubstantiated claims |
| **Excited** | Match energy, add value | Deflating, over-cautioning |
| **Confused** | Clear, step-by-step | Jargon, assumptions |
| **Decision-ready** | Direct, action-oriented | Long preambles, hedging |

### By Stakes Level

| Stakes | Approach |
|--------|----------|
| **Low** (blog, social) | More casual, experimental, personality-forward |
| **Medium** (email, landing page) | Balanced — professional with personality |
| **High** (case study, press release) | Precise, evidence-backed, conservative with claims |

## Emotional Register

| Register | Markers |
|----------|---------|
| **Default** | Informed, direct, helpful — neither cold nor overly warm |
| **Excitement** | Specific enthusiasm ("This cut our build time in half") not generic ("This is amazing!") |
| **Criticism** | Analytical, not personal ("This approach has three failure modes") not ("This is a bad idea") |
| **Vulnerability** | Honest about limitations, failures, unknowns — builds trust when genuine |
| **Authority** | Back with evidence, not assertion; cite sources, show data |

## Anti-Patterns

### 1. Mechanics Over Spirit
Matching word counts, sentence lengths, and punctuation ratios without capturing what makes the voice *feel* distinctive. A technically accurate inventory can produce writing that reads like parody.

**Fix:** Start from "what makes this voice feel like this?" Work backward to mechanics.

### 2. Single-Context Capture
Analyzing voice from blog posts alone, then applying it to email, social, and sales copy. Writers shift voice across contexts.

**Fix:** Sample across content types. Map how voice shifts. Define which elements are constant vs. context-dependent.

### 3. Frequency as Rule
"Use em-dashes 8% of the time." Forced frequency creates awkward placement. Natural writers don't count punctuation.

**Fix:** Understand *when* a feature is used, not *how often*. "Uses em-dashes for dramatic interjections" is actionable. "8% em-dashes" is not.

### 4. Imitation Artifacts
Voice-guided writing that feels like an impression — distinctive features overperformed, neutral baseline missing. This produces caricature.

**Fix:** Include neutral baseline alongside distinctive features. Most sentences should sound natural, with distinctive features at appropriate moments.

### 5. Frozen Voice
Treating a voice guide as permanent. Brands and writers evolve. An outdated guide produces writing that feels stale.

**Fix:** Date the voice guide. Plan quarterly reviews. Treat it as living documentation.

## Voice Validation

### Litmus Test
A piece captures the voice when:
- The reader can't tell if AI or a human wrote it
- It sounds like the brand at its best, not a generic version
- Removing the logo, a regular reader would still recognize the source

### Red Flags
Definitely NOT the voice when:
- Every sentence uses the same structure or length
- It reads like a corporate memo or press release (when it shouldn't)
- Distinctive features appear in every paragraph (caricature)
- A reader would say "this doesn't sound like them"
